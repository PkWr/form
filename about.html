About Form

This hole is a line some time next week.

A generative tool based on a publication by Patrick Walker.

Summary

Form turns the one rule of a publication, 11 ways I can make you up, into a tool. A block on a grid moves and the place it left stays marked. Draw a shape, or take one at random, and Form finds every way that rule can make it up. The rule is a sentence in the control panel ("35 ways I can make you up"): tap the words to choose Move, Change, where one block becomes another shape in the same place, or Grow, where the shape is built up from nothing one block at a time, to choose how many blocks act in one step (one, two, three or all of them), and to choose what follows: unmake you (the default), another rule, or nothing. It also runs the rule the other way, finding every way to unmake the shape one block at a time. Both come with every shape, in one sequence. The results play as an animation, lay out as a contact sheet and save as SVG artwork, a GIF or a video, and can be bound as an A5 book, imposed and ready to print. Every pattern has a short code, so the same code always makes the same book. It works in a web browser on a phone or a laptop, with no account and nothing uploaded.

Description

The book starts with one letter, a T built from square blocks, and asks how many ways a single form can be remade by moving its parts. Form starts from any shape: a square, an arch, a half square triangle or a circle, placed and turned on a grid of 5, 7 or 9, or a starter picked at random: a symbol (including signs in the manner of Rudolf Koch's The Book of Signs) or a character from a small 5×5 pixel typeface of capitals and numbers, drawn for Form.

The rule is the book's one rule. A block moves to a new place, stays joined to the rest, and the place it left stays marked. Everything in the book comes from that one move, shown in different ways. Form has all five:

iForm: the T and the variations made by moving one block.

iiDirection: each block is drawn as stripes that run along the line it is joined on.

iiiCumulative Solid: the blocks that moved leave a solid trace behind.

ivCumulative Transparency: the earlier variants stay behind as fading ghosts, so tone builds up wherever the same place was filled more than once.

vCumulative Density: every block is drawn in fine lines and each iteration adds a layer at its own angle, so where layers overlap the lines cross into a darker mesh.

Form also runs the rule the other way. The title says make you up; Unmake says unmake you. It takes blocks away one at a time, in an order fixed by the pattern, until the shape is gone.

Three ideas sit behind it. The first is the Stations of the Cross: one figure, a fixed sequence of stops, each one a pause. The second is Paul Klee's way of describing drawing as taking a line for a walk (Pedagogical Sketchbook, 1925); here a block takes the walk, and its trace is the line left behind. The third is Karl Gerstner's Designing Programmes, in which a design is set out as a rule that generates a field of related outcomes, not one fixed result.

Using it

Pick a shape, or take a symbol or a letter at random with Random symbol and Random character. Choose the rule (Move, Change or Grow) by tapping the sentence in the control panel; Unmake follows by default. Set how many blocks act at a time: one, two, three or all. Click the grid to place blocks (click a placed block again to turn it, and after four turns it is removed), then press the button, which reads whatever rule is chosen, for example "Make you up and unmake you". There are four steps: Draw, Animation, Contact sheet and Book. The animation downloads as an animated SVG, a looping GIF or a video for social media. The contact sheet downloads as an SVG on a portrait page, A3 to A0, ready to use as artwork. The grid size (5, 7 or 9) and the animation speed are in the control panel.

The book is an A5 publication in the design of the original, as a PDF. Imposed puts two pages on each SRA4 landscape sheet in booklet order, with bleed and crop marks: print double sided at full size, flip on the short edge, fold, bind and trim to the marks. Pages gives the plain A5 pages for reading on screen.

Pattern code

Every pattern has a code, such as 32753-KSWXQ-J9, shown in the control panel and printed on the last page of the book as "Pattern code". It records the shape, cropped to its bounding box, and the grid size, with a check at the end so a mistyped code is caught. The same shape drawn in a different place on the same grid gives the same code. Nothing in the book is left to chance: the order of the unmaking is fixed by the code and no date is printed, so entering a code, or opening the site with ?code= and the code, makes the identical book again. Two readers can compare codes to learn whether they hold the same book. The number of possible patterns is vast, so a hand-drawn one is in practice one of a kind; the starter symbols and very simple shapes are the exceptions, and they share codes.

How it's made

Form has been built since 31 October 2026. It is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no server, no login and nothing to install. Every shape is drawn in SVG, so the artwork is vector and prints at any size. The GIF encoder and the PDF writer for the book are written by hand, so nothing leaves the browser.

Making it was a collaboration. Patrick Walker brought the publication and the decisions about what the tool should do and how it should look; an AI coding assistant did the building, working from his direction. The AI is in the making, not the running: every shape and view is a drawing technique in code, not a generative model.

Energy & sustainability

Form runs on the device already in front of the person using it, a phone or a laptop. It works in the browser with no server, no render pass and no exported video, and what it draws is light vector shapes. Nothing is uploaded.

AI was used to build the tool, not to run it. Its energy was spent once, in development, and not again each time Form is used. The energy of building Form has not been measured separately.

Privacy

Form has no accounts, no sign-in, no cookies, no ads and no profiles of visitors. It stores nothing in your browser. It runs in your web browser and has no server of its own. The shapes you draw stay on your device, and the SVG files are made in the browser and saved straight to it.

Visit counts. Form counts visits with GoatCounter, a privacy-friendly service that sets no cookies and does not follow people across sites. It records which page was viewed, the page you came from, and general details such as browser, screen size and country, and keeps no personal profile. The shapes and patterns you make are never sent to it.

Hosting. The site is served by GitHub Pages. Like any web host, GitHub may record basic technical details of visits, such as IP address and browser; see GitHub's privacy statement. The code and fonts come from this site.
